Artaxias I Artaxiad A tier

Արտաշես Ա Արտաշեսյան

c. 190–160 BC

Why A tier: Artashes remains one of the best-known Armenian kings today, and that fame rests on something concrete: founding Artaxata (Արտաշատ) and the economic and land reforms that went with it. He was a builder and reformer first — not a schemer or scholar — and on stewardship alone he ranks among the greatest sovereigns in Armenian history, even when the other attributes look ordinary.

  • 14Allied with other Armenian rulers in Greater Armenia (Մեծ Հայք). Chief tie: Zariadres of Sophene (Զարեհ Երվանդունի, Ծոփք). After independence, no major wars with Rome (Հռոմ) or Parthia (Պարթևաստան). Solid, not spectacular.
  • 15Freed Paytakaran (Փայտակարան) from Media (Մարաստան). Defeated the Georgians; recovered Gugark (Գուգարք). Routed Alans (ալաններ) through the Caucasus; took an Alan prince prisoner. At the Kur (Կուր), equal armies — c. 80,000 Alan dead, c. 20,000 Armenian.
  • 20Founded Artaxata (Արտաշատ) on the Ararat plain — Arax (Արաքս) and Metsamor (Մեծամոր) — with Hannibal’s (Հաննիբալ) counsel. “Armenian Carthage.” Land reforms easing great landowners and village communities; regulated private landholding.
  • 48Little on plotting or scholarship. A Hellenistic court may have patronised artists and learned men — that is about all.

Tigranes II Artaxiad A tier

Տիգրան Բ Արտաշեսյան

95–55 BC

Why A tier: Many Armenians still treat him as the ultimate S-tier king because he built the largest empire in Armenian history. However, that empire collapsed within decades, crushed between Parthia (Պարթևաստան) and Rome (Հռոմ). His martial and stewardship achievements are legendary, but diplomatic miscalculations doomed the empire. He earns a solid A tier for his unmatched peak, even if his legacy was shorter-lived than others.

  • 10His diplomacy was bold but flawed. The Seleucid empire was exhausted by internal dynastic wars. To prevent total ruin, the Syrian (Ասորիք) nobility invited Tigranes to rule them in 84 BC. To secure his borders against Rome (Հռոմ) and Parthia (Պարթևաստան), he allied with Pontus (Պոնտոս).

    He bravely refused the Roman general Lucullus's (Լյուցիոս Լուկուլլոս) demand to surrender the defeated Pontic king. Yet, these moves backfired. The Armenian-Pontic alliance could not defeat the Rome-Parthia axis in the long run. Even his own son, Tigranes the Younger (Տիգրան Կրտսեր), rebelled at a critical moment. Nerve, but no lasting coalitions.
  • 19His battlefield record is nearly flawless. He conquered the Seventy Valleys (Յոթանասուն հովիտներ), seized Media (Մարաստան), and humbled Parthia (Պարթևաստան) to claim the title King of Kings (արքայից արքա).

    He won decisively at Damascus (Դամասկոս) and later shattered the Roman army at Aratsani (Արածանի). He did lose Tigranocerta (Տիգրանակերտ) when Lucullus outflanked him. However, his overall military peak remains unmatched in Armenian history.
  • 18He managed a massive, booming economy. Conquest multiplied Greater Armenia (Մեծ Հայք). He moved his court to Antioch (Անտիոք) to appease the Syrian elites. Realising the empire needed a capital on Armenian soil, he built the magnificent Tigranocerta (Տիգրանակերտ). (I once turned his capital-placement dilemma into a simple graph problem on SPOJ.)

    He controlled trade routes from Phoenicia (Փյունիկիա) to the east. He also boosted the economy by establishing "free cities" (ազատ քաղաքներ) with internal autonomy. He built for scale.
  • 10He was a straightforward ruler, not a cunning schemer. He successfully managed a vast multi-ethnic court, but was consistently outplayed in the shadows. Rome won the long game through subversion, and the gates of Tigranocerta were opened by traitors from within.
  • 12He brought Hellenistic culture to the elite, but it never reached the masses. Movses Khorenatsi (Մովսես Խորենացի) notes his Greek (հունարեն) education. Plutarch (Պլուտարքոս) describes thriving Greek theatres in Artashat (Արտաշատ) and Tigranocerta.

    The court even tried to merge Armenian and Greek gods, equating Aramazd (Արամազդ) with Zeus (Զևս) and Anahit (Անահիտ) with Artemis (Արտեմիս). However, this was purely a court fashion. The common people rejected it, limiting his cultural impact.

Artavasdes II Artaxiad A tier

Արտավազդ Բ Արտաշեսյան

55–34 BC

Why A tier: A playwright-king who wrote tragedies in Greek, balanced Rome (Հռոմ) and Parthia (Պարթևաստան) for years, then was betrayed by Antony, paraded in golden chains, and executed by Cleopatra. Total 71 — exceptional mind and solid diplomacy, dragged down by military caution and a catastrophic end.

  • 14Kept independence between two superpowers until the Antony disaster.
  • 11Abandoned Antony’s Parthian campaign — militarily cautious to a fault.
  • 13Maintained the realm through a dangerous era, without major building feats.
  • 15Survived by reading both empires; undone ultimately by trusting Antony’s safe-conduct.
  • 18Plutarch cites his Greek tragedies and histories — the highest Learning score in the list.

Tiridates I Arsacid B tier

Տրդատ Ա Արշակունի

63–88 AD

Why B tier: Founder of Arsacid Armenia and a Zoroastrian magus whose coronation by Nero in Rome (Հռոմ) — after a journey with 3,000 retainers — is one of Tacitus’s great set-pieces. Total 68 — a diplomatic masterpiece, but not a builder or conqueror on Tigranes’s scale.

  • 16The Nero coronation settlement ended decades of Roman–Parthian war over Armenia.
  • 13Magus first, general second; power came from negotiation.
  • 13Established a dynasty that lasted centuries.
  • 12Navigated Parthian and Roman interests without major scandal.
  • 14Religious learning as a magus counts here.

Tiridates III Arsacid B tier

Տրդատ Գ Արշակունի

c. 298–330 AD

Why B tier: Made Armenia the first Christian state (trad. 301 AD), converted by Gregory the Illuminator — the founding event of Armenian Christian historiography. Total 68 — civilisational importance is unmatched, but the CK3 stats reflect a pious warrior-king rather than a universal genius.

  • 14Recovered his throne with Roman help and then steered a religious revolution.
  • 16Tradition credits him with legendary physical strength and battlefield prowess.
  • 14Laid the institutional groundwork for a Christian kingdom.
  • 11Agathangelos frames him as a convert, not a schemer.
  • 13Patron of the Church more than a scholar himself.

Khosrov II Kotak Arsacid C tier

Խոսրով Բ Կոտակ Արշակունի

330–339 AD

Why C tier: Son of Tiridates III who inherited a newly Christian Armenia and ruled briefly before Persian intrigue removed him — one of the thinnest biographies on the list, and Roman sources often confuse him with his grandfather Khosrov I. Total 55 — included because the Arsacid succession matters, but the evidence barely supports a higher score on any axis.

  • 12Persian pressure and court plotting ended the reign; the one axis where the sources hint at a story.
  • 12101110Nine years on the throne with almost no independent record in Pavstos Buzand or Movses Khorenatsi — a placeholder king between two giants, not a builder, conqueror, or scholar.

Arshak II Arsacid C tier

Արշակ Բ Արշակունի

c. 350–368 AD

Why C tier: The defiant king of the long war against Shapur II; founded Arshakavan for refugees, clashed with nakharars and Church, and died in the Castle of Oblivion after a treacherous parley. Total 60 — heroic martyr-king whose martial valour could not compensate for diplomatic catastrophe.

  • 16Pavstos Buzand’s whole Book IV — relentless resistance against Persia.
  • 13Court and Church conflicts; outplotted at the end.
  • 101110Diplomatic catastrophe, a bleeding realm despite Arshakavan, and no scholarly legacy — heroic martyr, not a rounded ruler.

Pap Arsacid B tier

Պապ Արշակունի

370–374 AD

Why B tier: Restored autonomy after Arshak II’s imprisonment, curbed Church power, and was assassinated by the Romans at a banquet — narrated in granular detail by Ammianus Marcellinus. Total 62 — sharp and ruthless, but his story ends at a Roman dinner table.

  • 12Played Rome and Persia until Rome decided he was too independent.
  • 13Fought to restore the crown; competent, not legendary.
  • 12Brief reign of recovery, cut short.
  • 16Executed Catholicos Nerses, defied Rome — then fell to a classic trap.
  • 9No scholarly reputation.

Vramshapuh Arsacid C tier

Վրամշապուհ Արշակունի

389–417 AD

Why C tier: Peaceful king whose reign is remembered for one civilisational event: Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet (c. 405) with his patronage. Total 61 — immortal through Mashtots, but personally a mild steward rather than a powerhouse ruler.

  • 13Kept Armenia stable between Rome and Persia.
  • 9A reign of peace, not campaigns.
  • 13Patronage that enabled the alphabet — indirect but lasting.
  • 8Quiet court; little scheming recorded.
  • 18Alphabet patronage is the highest Learning event in Armenian history.

Ashot I Bagratid B tier

Աշոտ Ա Բագրատունի

884–890

Why B tier: Restored the Armenian crown after centuries of foreign rule, recognised as king by both the Abbasid Caliph and Byzantine Emperor — the Bagratid restoration begins here. Total 67 — indispensable restorer, but a short reign limits the score ceiling.

Smbat I Bagratid C tier

Ashot II Bagratid C tier

Աշոտ Բ Բագրատունի

914–929
